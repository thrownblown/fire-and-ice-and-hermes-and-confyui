# Stone Age filmmaking — podcast interview reference

External reference, captured 2026-09-29. Not Fire & Ice source material. It is here
because it is a working filmmaker talking about the specific problems of making an
ambitious period film with almost no money, and because the host is a historian of
film who keeps pushing on the question our project keeps running into: what does an
audience actually require before it will sit through a quiet film.

Roughly 45 minutes of interview, filler and crosstalk removed. The guest is Martin
Peltz, from Utrecht, who has actually made the films rather than writing about them.
The host is a film writer whose channel runs on patron donations. The transcript
itself names neither of them — the guest's name appears only in the host's opening
line — so the attribution here comes from that line, not from the conversation.

## The two films

**The Bison's Legs** — a short, late Paleolithic, inspired by French cave art. Peltz
wrote the script, ran a crowdfunding, partly funded and partly self-funded, and made
it. The production ran on borrowed resources in a way that reads as a strategy rather
than a compromise: artifacts were borrowed from experimental archaeologists, and the
Dutch National Forestry Association gave guidance and location access in one of their
nature reserves for free. He paid the actors a token amount. Crew and cast together
numbered about eight people.

**The Eternal Seasons** — the planned feature, and the reason for the interview.
Late Paleolithic, roughly 15,000 years ago, southwestern Europe, which was then
relatively warm and had a mix of steppe and broadleaf forest rather than being all
tundra. A young woman wants to become a mother and cannot; she knows the reason and
that is the tension. Four seasons across one life. He is explicit that it will not be
people sitting under trees — there is a story, some jokes, jealousy, rivalry. His
interests in film came first; the period came second, chosen because the art of that
era implies a sophistication the popular depiction keeps denying.

## The archaeology, briefly

Late Paleolithic, no writing, so the argument runs on inference from objects and from
ethnographic analogy. Venus figurines span more than 20,000 years from western France
to eastern Siberia, which Peltz reads as evidence of shared culture. Weaving is
inferred from impressions on fired clay in Spain, and he makes the cheap-argument case
for it: reeds, interleave, done, versus the hundred processes a sheep-based textile
industry needs. Soft material culture is the hard part — hundreds of thousands of
flint spearheads against roughly ten surviving wooden spears, the Neanderthal ones
from Schöningen. At Solutré in France, hundreds of square metres of horse bone, which
prompted the theory (Paul Bahn's) that horses may have been kept and herded much
earlier than assumed, initially for meat. He wants to avoid dramatising that kind of
theory explicitly, because a theory spanning thousands of years cannot be honestly
compressed into a story about one year.

## The disagreements

**Harmony with nature.** Peltz offers a two-part definition: do not exhaust or poison
the environment, and know the environment thoroughly enough to act on it — which
berries grow where in which part of the season, what the animals are doing across the
year, what to do with every part of an animal. The host rejects the premise outright.
A hunter-gatherer who stampedes a herd off a cliff wastes 98% of it; one who burns a
forest to drive game out is not practising custodial restraint. He raises soil
analysis and fertiliser as proof that farmers are the ones managing land
sustainably. Peltz concedes that "they spent 99% of their time not fighting each other"
and that violence is genuinely a leading cause of death for hunter-gatherer men, while
insisting the era has been sold to us as uniformly brutal. The host wins the
definition; the factual concession is Peltz's.

**Violence as default content.** Every Stone Age film the host has seen contains
extreme violence. Peltz's short was a dream quest with none, and his defence is that a
non-violent day in that period certainly happened. The host does not dispute this and
immediately explains why it is beside the point: a meaningful script meets a producer
whose job is raising money, and that producer asks where the action is.

**Language.** Peltz used modern Dutch in the short because there was no time to build
a reconstructed language, and the words were deliberately kept out of modern idiom. He
prefers a reconstruction for the feature. The host thinks it is a poor trade —
invented speech reads as gibberish to almost any viewer — and Peltz agrees it is a lot
of work for little realism while maintaining it aids world-building. Peltz's practical
answer is the useful one: memorise lines so a character can repeat them, because
repetition is what makes invented speech read as a real language.

**Structure versus deviation.** The host argues for classical form and Peltz agrees
wholeheartedly: beginning, middle, end, characters changed by what happens to them,
conflict between characters. A group of twenty-five interdependent people generates
that conflict for free; you cannot hide in a crowd the way you can in a city. Both
land on the same conclusion from opposite directions — you have to keep it
interesting, and you have to do that inside a form the audience already knows.

## What this means for Fire & Ice

Useful:

- **Borrowed material is a design decision, not a shortfall.** Peltz's artifacts came
  from experimental archaeologists because that is who actually makes them. Our
  reference stills are frame-exact slices of the film we are remaking, which is the
  same move taken one step further: the source is already the best possible reference,
  and the interesting question is what we add on top of it, not where we find
  references.
- **An audience's need for movement is a constraint, not a compromise.** The producer
  asking "where's the action" is the same force we are meeting in the renders. T0004
  cut frozen-frame fraction across every chunk by writing real motion into the prompt
  and the clips came out flat, and T0005 answered it by escalating to body-scale
  gesture. The interview is a useful check that this is the right axis to keep pushing
  on rather than a quirk of our prompts.
- **Classical structure has to exist somewhere above the unit of generation.** Peltz
  is shooting one year of a life and worrying about a three-act shape; we are
  generating five-second chunks and worrying about nothing above them. His argument is
  the argument for maintaining the beat sheet as a real spine rather than treating
  chunking as a purely mechanical step.
- **Authenticity versus spectacle is a choice the model will make for you if you do
  not.** He is arguing against 10,000 BC's harnessed mammoths — plausible-looking
  invention that is not in the evidence. H3 will happily invent a detail we never
  specified. Our discipline of writing only what is visible in the keyframes is the
  same commitment, and the frame metrics are how we check we held it.
- **Casting continuity is what carries identity when nothing else can.** Eight people
  in his film, unpaid, is a manageable consistency problem. 494 chunks generated
  independently is not, which is why the curated character traits override whatever
  the vision model reports about a face.

Does not transfer:

- His economics are volunteer labour and borrowed access. Ours is one GPU and
  twenty-seven hours. The scarcity is inverted, so the interesting constraint moves:
  when compute is the bottleneck, the question is what is worth 0.9 seconds a frame,
  not what can be cut.
- Reconstructed language and dialogue authenticity are close to irrelevant here. We
  inherit the original English dialogue and want to keep it verbatim; his hardest
  problem is the one we get for free.
- The museum-reconstruction argument (concede a little uncertainty to make the story
  land) is a live question for us precisely because our source is not uncertain. The
  model invents; we decide how much invention to keep.
